Rapid deep‑space transit is a heat management problem.

In an open‑cycle nuclear rocket every joule that becomes waste heat is a joule that did not become thrust — managing the heat and making the thrust are the same act, not competing ones. Switch the ideas below on and off in any order: across all 32 combinations, the energy that ends up inside the ship predicts the wet mass with a correlation of r = 0.97 over eleven orders of magnitude. The bare fission‑fragment rocket needs 3.4×10¹⁰ t for 7.5 light‑years in 97 years; the same mission with the heat routed into the exhaust instead of the hull needs 6.5×10³ t.

There is no exception on this page. The trajectory is a thermal lever: burning impulsively rather than continuously halves the rapidity budget, which cuts the propellant, which cuts the fission, which cuts the heat — 244 PW into the hull becomes 80 TW, a factor of three thousand. And the fragment speed is the first and largest thermal decision of all. A thermal rocket must convert all of its energy into heat before any of it becomes thrust, which caps the exhaust at what materials survive: hydrogen at 5,000 K gives 6.4 km/s. Fission fragments leave at 8,994 km/s because the reaction products are the exhaust and were never thermalised — a factor of 1,395 obtained by refusing to make heat in the first place. Fusion needs a fission trigger and fission backing to sustain it; antimatter costs more to make than it returns and delivers its energy as isotropic gammas that cannot be collimated. Direct fission, or fission‑driven fusion, is the only route to a relativistic exhaust — and without a relativistic exhaust the question of human interstellar transit does not arise at all.

How the five levers work together: Coasting optimisation and MHD collimation are the primary drivers of propellant mass reduction. Ablator coupling (η) further lowers propellant mass, while thin‑film radiators remove the residual heat that remains after these mechanisms have done their work.
